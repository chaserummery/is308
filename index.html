Research Journey

Four Labs. One Awakening.

I came into this course thinking I already understood technology. I used five platforms daily, studied design, was building my own website. What I didn't realise was how much those systems understood — and shaped — me.

01
Digital Identity

Who Am I Online?

Mapping my footprint across Instagram, TikTok, WeChat, YouTube, and Amazon revealed I perform a different version of myself on every platform. The algorithm doesn't meet you — it constructs you. Benjamin's "coded exposure" made visible what I'd always felt but never named.

02
Algorithmic Bias

How Do Algorithms See Us?

I tested "CEO" across three search engines. Results were almost entirely white men. Adding "Black" shifted results from professional profiles to discussions about race itself. The search engine claimed neutrality. It was anything but. Noble's Algorithms of Oppression stopped being theory.

03
Classification Systems

Who Gets Counted?

Analysing UIUC's course catalog revealed a system built on assumptions — STEM over art, Western history as default, non-Western knowledge as "special topics." Transferring from Studio Art to Information Sciences, I felt this not as theory but as the actual terrain I was navigating.

04
Accessibility Audit

Who Can Access This Space?

I audited Canvas, YouTube, and the UIUC library site. All three offered accessibility features on paper. None worked well for international students or non-native English speakers. The sentence I kept returning to: accessible and actually usable are not the same thing.

Critical Discoveries

Three Things That Changed How I See.

1

Neutral Is a Lie

Every system I investigated presented itself as objective. None were. Benjamin's New Jim Code gave me the language: discrimination hides inside an autocomplete suggestion, a ranking algorithm, a default database field. The most dangerous inequalities are the ones that feel like natural order.

Benjamin — New Jim Code
2

Available ≠ Accessible

There is a difference between adding a ramp to the side of a building and designing the whole thing inclusive from the start. Canvas has screen reader support. YouTube has auto-captions. But the library has no translation option. As a future UX designer, I'll always ask: who was in the room when these decisions were made?

Lab 4 — Design Justice
3

My Body Was Already Data

Growing up mixed-race in China, I was already outside the system's default categories before I ever used a smartphone. Systems built around a default user treat everyone else as edge cases. TikTok claiming to protect minors while mining their data is the same logic applied everywhere — safety framing conceals extraction.

Personal Connection

My Identity Is the Data.

"I was mixed-race in China before I was a data point in an algorithm — but the feeling was the same. The system wasn't built with me in mind."
Mixed Race Born UK / Raised China F1 Visa International Student UX Designer iSchool Transfer

In my Universal Design discussion post, I wrote about real-time translation earbuds as the next autocorrect — a tool that seems niche until it isn't. Growing up between three countries, I have always lived in the gap between languages. This course gave me a framework for why that gap is political, not just personal.

Transferring from Studio Art to Information Sciences is a commitment to being the person in the room who asks: who are we leaving out? I applied to Dr. Adler's accessibility research lab because I want to build the ramp into the foundation, not bolt it on afterward.

My F1 visa means I can only work on campus. Classification systems — visa categories, enrollment codes, course prerequisites — shape what I can do every single day. You don't have to intend exclusion. You just have to not notice it.

Community Engagement Vision

What I'm Carrying Forward.

This course ends. The questions don't. I started the semester thinking I understood technology because I used it fluently. I finish it understanding that fluency in a system is not the same as understanding how that system works — or who it was built to serve.

I now see design choices as political choices. Default settings as statements about who matters. The absence of a translation option as a decision, not an oversight. As someone heading into UX and information science, the things I build will either replicate these patterns or resist them.

  • Research: Pursuing accessibility work with Dr. Adler's lab — generative AI health app with equity built in from the start.

  • Design practice: Every project will include accessibility testing with real users — not just automated checks, not as an afterthought.

  • Keep reading: Benjamin's Imagination: A Manifesto is next. More time with resistance and joy, not just harm documentation.

  • Multilingual design: Treating multilingual interfaces as a first-class design constraint, not a feature request.

  • Community: Bringing these conversations to Lego Club and my basketball team — spaces where people don't usually talk about algorithmic bias, which is exactly why they should.
